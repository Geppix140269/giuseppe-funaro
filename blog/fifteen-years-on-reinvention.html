Fifteen Years On: What Leaving Corporate Life Taught Me About Reinvention

Around fifteen years ago, I went through one of the most important transitions of my professional life. I could not see it clearly at the time. With distance, I understand it very differently.

For many years my career had followed a path that, from the outside, looked fairly conventional. Technology led to telecommunications. Telecommunications became international sales, market entry and eventually senior commercial leadership. I managed Country Managers across multiple countries, carried significant P&L responsibility and worked inside large, structured organisations.

Then that chapter ended.

I had spent so long operating inside companies with teams, reporting lines, budgets, targets and a clear place in the organisation that I underestimated how much of my professional identity was tied to that structure.

Leaving it was not simply a change of employer. It was the beginning of a much less linear period of my career.

Experience does not automatically give you direction

By then I knew how to build a business plan. I understood P&Ls, cash flow, pricing, sales organisations and commercial strategy. I had negotiated large agreements and managed businesses considerably larger than anything I was about to build myself.

What I did not have was a clear answer to a much simpler question: what exactly did I want to do next?

That distinction matters more than I appreciated at the time. Competence and direction are not the same thing.

There followed a period of trying things. Some ideas went nowhere. Some cost money and time. Some were useful because they taught me what I did not want to do. Eventually, one opportunity became a real operating business.

Three shops, six employees and close to €1 million in turnover

In the aftermath of the global financial crisis, gold-buying businesses began appearing across Spain and other parts of Europe. There was a real market, driven by economic pressure, high gold prices and people looking to release value from jewellery and other assets.

We opened our first shop in Vilanova i la Geltrú.

It worked.

That gave us the confidence to open another location in Sitges and later a third in Vilafranca del Penedès. At its peak, the business had three retail locations, six employees and turnover approaching €1 million. We traded gold, jewellery, diamonds and other precious items, and I was involved in everything from purchasing and pricing to financing, staffing, sales and day-to-day operations.

It was very different from running a commercial organisation inside a multinational company. There was no distance between a decision and its consequence. A new location was not a line in a strategy presentation. It was a lease, salaries, working capital, inventory and another fixed-cost base.

The first location had worked well. The second did not perform in the same way. We still opened a third.

At the same time, larger and better-capitalised operators were entering the market. They could absorb volatility and compete with a financial strength that a small independent business simply did not have.

Eventually, the economics stopped working for us.

The lesson was not that cash flow matters

It would be easy to turn that story into a neat business-school lesson about cash flow, over-expansion or working capital.

That would miss the point.

I already understood those things intellectually. The more useful lesson came from experiencing how different decision-making becomes when you are personally inside the risk.

You can know how to analyse a business and still make poor decisions inside one.

Corporate experience gave me useful tools. It did not make me objective. It did not eliminate optimism. It did not stop a successful first location from making expansion feel more obvious than it really was.

And it did not remove the emotional difference between analysing someone else's business and making decisions with your own capital, reputation and future tied to the outcome.

Entrepreneurship is not a smaller version of corporate management

Large organisations contain a great deal of invisible infrastructure. Finance teams, controls, colleagues, processes, budgets and governance all create friction around decisions. That friction can be frustrating, but it is also protection.

In a small company much of that disappears.

The founder can decide quickly. That is one of the great advantages of entrepreneurship. It is also one of the risks.

There is nobody automatically standing between conviction and action.

Looking back, that period changed how I think about evidence, assumptions and downside scenarios. It made me much more interested in the question that sits underneath any growth plan: what would have to be true for this decision to be wrong?

Reinvention rarely looks like reinvention while you are living it

The years that followed were not a clean return to a single career track.

I created a publishing business in Sitges. I returned to executive assignments. I worked in transport, international procurement and commodities. During COVID I built an international PPE sourcing and distribution operation that generated close to €10 million in revenue. More recently I have worked on trade intelligence, AI-enabled products and new digital ventures.

When you put those things on a page fifteen years later, it is tempting to draw a straight line between them.

There was no straight line.

That may be the part of reinvention that is most difficult to explain, particularly to people earlier in their careers. We tend to look backwards at successful careers and assume the person living them knew where the story was going.

Often they did not.

You can have experience and still be uncertain. You can have been successful before and still have to prove yourself again. You can make mistakes without everything that came before becoming worthless.

And sometimes what looks like a detour while you are living it becomes part of the experience you rely on later.

What looks different after fifteen years

I do not look back at that retail business as the moment when I learned how to read a balance sheet. It was not.

I look at it as the point when I began to understand something more difficult: professional experience does not remove uncertainty, and previous success does not guarantee good judgement in a new context.

Those are lessons I still use.

They matter when evaluating a new market. They matter when looking at a new venture. They matter when deciding whether growth is real or simply activity. And they matter when a career changes direction unexpectedly.

Fifteen years later, I no longer see that period as a break from my career.

It became part of the career.
